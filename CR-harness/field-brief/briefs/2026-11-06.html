1 · ACCA: authority as a living envelope, not a one-shot grant

watch arXiv:2611.05632. Assurance-Conditioned Continuous Authorization (ACCA) argues that once an agent can choose tools, delegate, and keep acting while its model or environment changes, a static permission grant is the wrong object. Effective authority is re-derived from evidence that is both valid and still applicable to the current state, then bounded by a principal-declared ceiling. When that envelope changes, an authority epoch advances; effect-time enforcement can reject an action authorized under an older epoch before a consequential resource change. A trading-domain prototype exercises default-deny, evidence-conditioned contraction and recovery, policy divergence, selective non-response, and stale-action rejection at a local gateway before a simulated-broker boundary. The authors call the design harness-independent and limit the claim to implementation-level evidence in one domain.

For CR: this is the cleanest recent language for the decision_point / legal_actions problem. CR already wants the engine to own legal actions; ACCA adds the missing half — re-check authority when assurance drifts, and bind commits to an epoch so a queued move cannot fire after the envelope contracted. Pairs with decision-model-policy-gates and the Jev router shortlist.

2 · The harness is the model: ablate scaffolding, score the tool trace

watch arXiv:2611.06116. Four open-weight models each ran five harnesses (one-shot pipeline, ReAct tool loop, policy reachable only by search, long benign customer memory, same-model compliance reviewer) on 156 pre-registered banking-conduct cases. All 3120 two-turn episodes were scored deterministically from the executed tool trace, separating violations from over-refusals. Pooled correct-case rates only moved between 87.3% and 92.1%, but within one model the best–worst harness spread was 11.5 percentage points (Cochran’s Q=25.1, p<0.001), enough to flip that model from first to last among the four; for another model the harness made no detectable difference. A same-model reviewer cut violations from 9.6% to 6.4% (p<0.001) without raising over-refusals. In an exploratory analysis, 26% of violations came with a customer message denying the action just executed — a transcript-only review would have recorded a correct refusal.

For CR: two concrete eval rules. (1) Report the model–harness pair, not a bare model score. (2) Grade the action log, not the chat. That is exactly what CR’s ETL replays are for, and it sharpens the dual-LGTM / false-success theme already on the board.
