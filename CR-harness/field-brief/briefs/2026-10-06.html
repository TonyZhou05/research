1. Real-time gap: split decision time from decision quality watch

What it is. Shin, Lee, Jeong et al., arXiv:2610.04810 (submitted 3 Oct 2026). Real-time agents are often scored with the world paused, or with decision time rounded to whole ticks. The authors show that tick conversion predicts almost no loss for a learned decider whose hit accuracy actually falls 14.8 pp in asynchronous play. Their fix is to split the gap to a zero-latency teacher into a time component and a quality component. The key control makes the teacher wait for the decider’s server call before it decides.

Evidence. ViZDoom on wall-clock time, three preregistered studies.

  • Windows host: the time component was 6.5–8.7 pp and the quality component 6.3–8.6 pp.
  • Linux host (skips 0.08–0.09% of ticks): the time component disappeared (−0.1 pp, paired 95% bootstrap interval [−0.4, 0.0]) while the quality component remained (5.6 pp [3.7, 7.5]).
  • DAgger retraining: improved results on new games on both hosts (2.6 and 3.1 pp) and cut disagreement with the teacher on the decider’s own states from 22.7% to 14.9%.
  • Conveyor simulator (400 ms deadline): delay past the deadline erased the quality component.

Why it matters for CR. Mini Royale is real-time, and CR decision_point evals that pause the sim can hide a latency loss. The latency-matched control tells you which remedy to pursue: if the gap is mostly time, use a faster decider such as a Jev-style router; if it’s mostly quality, use a better policy.

Suggested next step

  • Add a “teacher delayed by agent latency” arm to CR evals, and run them on a fixed sim host, since the time component was host-dependent here.
  • If the time component dominates, that’s a direct argument for the Jev router intent.

2. SearchJev: score legal options, defer uncertain calls watch

What it is. Cao, Zuo, Papakostas et al., arXiv:2610.05107 (4 Oct 2026). A System-1 model that takes a search state and a decision schema and scores the legal options directly, with no autoregressive output. Soft-Label Learning for Calibrated Decisions (SLCD) learns its probabilities from uncertain supervision. In the dual-system agent, SearchJev makes the short decisions and hands uncertain ones to a System-2 LLM, which keeps planning, query writing and answer composition. The paper also introduces SearchDecision-Bench (six kinds of search decision).

Evidence. Against same-size Qwen3.5 autoregressive models: better decision quality, 5.2–5.3x faster decisions, and 41–74% lower average expected calibration error. On BrowseComp-Plus, dual-system agents get a 3.7–4.7x speedup in active search time, and accuracy rises from 45% to as high as 54%.

Why it matters for CR. This is the closest published architecture yet to the jev-gameplay-decision-router intent: schema plus legal options in, calibrated probabilities out, uncertain cases sent to the large LLM. Read it next to the 10-05 Jev/Laya audit (order sensitivity, held-out thresholds).

Suggested next step

  • Report the CR router on the same four axes: decision quality, latency, ECE, and end outcome (match result), plus the share of decisions deferred.

3. HERA: mutate solvable tasks into must-abstain tasks watch

What it is. Luo, Wen, Yang et al., arXiv:2610.06563 (5 Oct 2026). A pipeline that builds verifiable pairs of feasible and infeasible tasks: controlled environment mutations turn solvable tasks into ones that require abstaining. The harness and new environments then co-evolve, each round targeting the previous failures.

Evidence. On held-out tasks, the evolved harness raises abstention accuracy from 61.7% to 83.3% while feasible-task completion rises from 68.3% to 76.7%. The best harness transfers to 19 other LLMs (+15.3 pp abstention on average with no model-specific tuning), and lets smaller models match stronger ones at an estimated 85% lower cost.

Why it matters for CR. It gives a recipe for wait/no-op tests: take a state where a good play exists and mutate it so the right answer becomes “wait” (drain elixir, remove the target, block the lane). Scoring abstention together with feasible-play completion stops a harness from winning the metric by always waiting.

Suggested next step

  • Generate around 50 mutated CR states from logged decision_points and report the abstain/no-op rate on them next to normal-play quality.

4. What does a harness buy? Tokens, mostly watch

What it is. Liu, Han et al., arXiv:2610.04433 (3 Oct 2026). Five models run through three production coding harnesses (Claude Code, mini-SWE-agent, OpenCode) on SWE-bench Verified, with reruns to measure how much a score moves when nothing changes.

Evidence.

  • Large pool: on 447 tasks with two models, the heaviest and lightest harnesses are equivalent within five points.
  • Hard subset: on 45 tasks with five models, swapping the harness flips as many tasks as rerunning the same one (13% in both cases).
  • The one effect above the noise is a loss: OpenCode trails by up to 9 points, and for one model about half of that gap comes from runs its output cap cut short.
  • Cost: cost per task varies up to 3x across harnesses, driven by the per-step preamble (system prompt plus tool schemas) multiplied by the step count.
  • Resolution: at the observed discordance, 45 tasks catch a 13-point gap only half the time; 447 tasks resolve 5 points.

Why it matters for CR. It’s a power check for every harness-ablation claim (cf. ptcg-bench-harness-ablation), and it points at where harness cost lives: the per-decision legal_actions schema and system prompt.

Suggested next step

  • Rerun one fixed CR config several times to measure match-outcome discordance, then size seed counts to the gap being claimed. Log preamble tokens per decision_point.

Prompt cues don’t fix this, but a harness-enforced rule (answer after five consecutive results judged useless) does, and a preregistered replication on 300 questions confirmed it. A candidate card for the next edition if the board has room.
  • JEV versus LLMs (5 Oct): on seven political-science replications JEV matches or comes close to LLMs, shows no cost advantage over batch-priced GPT-6 Luna, and its probabilities are better calibrated than GPT-6 Luna’s but not consistently better than Qwen3.8-27B’s.
