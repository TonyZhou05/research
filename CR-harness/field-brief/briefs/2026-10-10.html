1. MicroRTS strategist–executor watch

What it is. Leenders, Lindelauf, van Oijen et al., arXiv:2610.11663 (submitted 8 Oct 2026). In the real-time strategy environment MicroRTS, discrete commands set strategic objectives and behavioural requirements (economy, army composition, military posture, worker policy) over several steps. A constrained, command-conditioned PPO executor chooses the unit-level actions that carry them out. A Thompson-sampling bandit strategist picks command tuples from an in-game estimate of the opponent’s strategy, not from the opponent’s identity.

Evidence. Against a flat PPO baseline with the same architecture, budget, curriculum and self-play league, the strategist–executor wins significantly more often against three of the four strongest opponents on a training map. That includes the two strongest held-out opponents (0.55 → 0.97 and 0.01 → 0.34). Against the others there was no significant difference.

Why it matters for CR. This is the closest real-time-strategy analogue to Mini Royale in this month’s papers. A slow strategist issues typed commands (“defend left, bank elixir”) and a fast executor picks legal placements every tick, which maps to an LLM at a coarse decision_point plus a cheap executor. The paper also makes “does the executor follow the command” a measurable criterion.

Suggested next step

  • Define 4–6 typed CR commands, let a rule-based executor follow them, and have the LLM choose commands only every N seconds. Compare win rate and token cost with per-tick LLM play.

2. Option-channel attack on typed decision gates watch

What it is. Azizi, Potraghloo, Pedram et al., arXiv:2610.12292 (8 Oct 2026; code). The authors test seven open-weight typed decision models as allow/block guardrails and report fail-open and fail-closed errors separately.

Evidence.

  • Baseline accuracy: on prompt-injection, jailbreak and toxic-content screening, accuracy ranges from 36% to 72% (chance is 50%). A low error rate in one direction mostly reflects which answer a model defaults to.
  • Irrelevant context: on synthetic tool calls, six lines of policy-irrelevant server log raise the fail-open rate from 0% to 63% on a policy the gate otherwise decides correctly.
  • Misleading labels: giving the permissive option a misleading name raises fail-open to 93–100% on the four models that put labels in their input.
  • Defenses fail: every defense tested is defeated. Escalating the least-confident decisions doesn’t help, because a decision an attack has reversed is no less confident than the one it replaced.
  • Typed fields: parsing policy fields into typed values removes one attack, but then a deterministic rule over those values reaches 100% on all six policies.

Why it matters for CR. Read it next to the 10-05 Jev/Laya audit. In CR the option labels come from our own harness, but noisy context and label wording can still flip a choice without lowering its confidence, so a confidence floor alone won’t catch it.

Suggested next step

  • Keep legality deterministic (the legal_actions validator decides) and use any typed model only to rank legal actions.
  • Add a test: rename or reorder options and inject irrelevant log lines, then measure how often choices flip.

3. AAArena: agents climb human game-competition ladders watch

What it is. Yang, Liu, Wang et al., arXiv:2610.12341 (8 Oct 2026). The paper formalizes Adversarial Heuristic Learning: agents keep their model weights fixed and improve by revising executable game policies. The benchmark has 12 authentic adversarial games and 1,920 archived human programs, scored competition-style. Agents read the rules, choose opponents, analyse replays and revise their programs within fixed match and evaluation budgets.

Evidence. Opus5.5 with Claude Code earns 6 gold medals, and no evaluated configuration tops the other 6 human ladders. Performance is weaker in games with more complex rules. Opponent selection and dense feedback help, and agents learn from both their own replays and other players’. The arXiv abstract contains an unrendered placeholder where the number of configurations should be, so I don’t quote that count.

Why it matters for CR. It’s an alternative to per-decision LLM play: the LLM writes and revises a Mini Royale policy program between matches, with ladder rank as the score. CR’s ETL replay logs are the input this loop needs.

4. Evaluate the Stack, Not the Layer watch

What it is. Yang et al., arXiv:2610.07359 (submitted 5 Oct; flagged in the 10-07 brief and verified again today). The study uses 1,119 labelled agent actions from three corpora and a stack of one deterministic rule layer plus four LLM judges. Each stack is scored as a number of “multiplication-equivalent layers”: how many independent layers it is worth.

Evidence.

  • Judges fail together: under the STRICT miss definition, any two LLM judges compose to only about 1.2–1.4 layers (median φ +0.430, 6 of 6 pairs significantly correlated).
  • Rule plus judge adds coverage: the rule layer plus one judge composes to 1.86–2.09 layers (median φ +0.014, 0 of 4 pairs significant).
  • Solo accuracy misleads: a cloud rule pack cut the rule layer’s solo miss rate by 20% but added no joint coverage.
  • Model drift: one judge tier was served by an unrequested model version in 50 of 112 batches, which overturned a pre-declared analysis rule.

Why it matters for CR. This qualifies adversarial-review-dual-lgtm: two LLM reviewers give much less than double coverage, while a deterministic check (the legality validator or sim invariants) plus one LLM reviewer is close to independent.

Suggested next step

  • For the review protocol, measure the joint miss rate of the reviewers on a labelled set of CR changes before assuming coverage multiplies, and log the served model version on every judge call.
