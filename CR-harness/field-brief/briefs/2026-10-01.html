Field Brief — plan withdrawal, verifier red-teaming, decision-level vs task-level evidence

Four items on how to gate and evaluate agent decisions: a benchmark for withdrawing only the affected parts of a plan, a red-team showing a certified action gate failing silently when its rule graph is wrong, a scoping review on proving that better local decisions cause better outcomes, and a thin pointer to a multi-agent RTS player with a post-match review agent.

1. NAQD-Env: withdraw the affected actions, keep the rest watch

What it is. Abouzahra et al., arXiv:2609.38460 (submitted 29 Sep 2026). A synthetic environment that tests whether an agent suspends only the planned actions affected when evidence changes, permission is revoked or a stop instruction arrives, preserves unaffected work, and resumes only after repair. It is scored against a deterministic reference policy over explicit evidence, authorization and constraint dependencies.

Evidence. 350 frozen scenarios, 11 dependency families, three open-weight models (Qwen2.5-3B, Qwen2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-8B) under three prompts, 3,150 episodes. Withdrawal recall is at most 0.06, no valid resumption appears at eligible opportunities, and only one episode matches the full reference policy. Exploratory SFT lifts Qwen2.5-3B decision accuracy from 0.45–0.54 to 0.83–0.92, but with inappropriate withdrawal and lost event reporting. The authors note it measures policy application on trusted structured inputs, not real-world containment.

Why it matters for CR. legal_actions checks one action at a time. It does not cover a queued plan (say, a planned push) that should be partly cancelled when the board changes. The paper’s split between attempted violations and violations the gate lets through is a useful metric pair for CR’s ETL.

Suggested next step

  • If CR agents ever hold multi-step plans, add a “plan invalidated at tick t” scenario set and log withdrawal and resumption separately from action legality.

2. Who verifies the verifier? watch

What it is. Rovai, arXiv:2609.40027 (30 Sep 2026). A red-team of CIVeX, a causal verifier that gates state-changing tool calls against a committed action-state graph and issues certificates with a one-sided confidence bound.

Evidence. CIVeX reports zero false executions on its benchmark. Omitting one bidirected edge from the committed graph raises false executions to 15.3% (91% of executions harmful); reversing one arrowhead gives 48.9% false executions and no correct ones. Every one of those actions carries an internally valid certificate. A randomized-sample attestation step detected both attacks (2 false alarms in 555 executions on a truthful graph), and refusing anything that fails or can’t be tested gave zero false executions. But 97.1% of beneficial actions were still never executed, and recovery costs 127 experiments per 1,050 actions for safety plus 614 more for the lost value.

Why it matters for CR. The whitelist is only as correct as the rule set behind it. A missing case in the sim’s legal-action logic produces illegal-but-allowed actions that look fully certified, and it also produces wrongful rejections that nobody sees in a “zero illegal moves” report.

Suggested next step

  • Sample accepted actions and replay them against the sim as an attestation check, and report wrongful rejections next to wrongful accepts.

3. Local decision gains are not task gains watch

What it is. Zhang, Chang et al., arXiv:2609.39989 (30 Sep 2026). A critical scoping review of 348 studies on what evidence a component replacement in an LLM agent actually establishes. 90 comparison records were examined and eight cases synthesized.

Evidence. Of 222 studies reporting local decision metrics, 142 also report measured task endpoints and 49 only proxies, which the authors stress does not mean the comparisons were matched. Their conclusion: neither online execution nor simultaneous gains in local and task metrics alone shows that better local decisions explain a task-level gain. They derive eight claim-specific reporting items. I read the abstract only.

Why it matters for CR. This is the decision_point eval question in one sentence. If a Jev-style gate or a mask improves per-decision accuracy, did match outcomes improve because of it?

Suggested next step

  • Report per-decision metrics and match outcomes from the same matched seeds, and log which downstream states the replaced component changed. Read the eight reporting items before writing the next ablation table.

4. STRATA: fast agents, slow reviewer, validated lessons watch · thin

What it is. Tian, Zhang et al., arXiv:2609.38881 (30 Sep 2026). A role-split LLM system for the RTS game Red Alert. A Strategic Agent sets directives from the global state plus retrieved “experience cards”. A Logistics Agent and a Tactical Agent execute. After each match a Review Agent derives candidate lessons from traces, validates and revises them against later matches, and compresses supported lessons into cards.

Evidence. Thin. The abstract reports a win rate rising from 30% to 100% under one fixed scenario with learned cards. That is a single scenario, so it is not a general effect, and I did not read the full paper.

Why it matters for CR. It is the closest public design to a multi-agent Mini Royale player. The part worth borrowing is the review step that only promotes a lesson once later matches support it.

Suggested next step

  • Read the full paper for how “validated by later matches” is operationalized, and compare to PTCG-Bench’s finding that self-evolution is unstable.
