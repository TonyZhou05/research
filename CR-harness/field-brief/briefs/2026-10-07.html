1. SAG: gate the critic on admissible-action ambiguity watch

What it is. Park, Im, Kwon et al., arXiv:2610.07335 (submitted 5 Oct 2026). SAG treats “should I call the critic now?” as a decision at every step. A training-free gate estimates the value of a critique from two ambiguity signals computed over the admissible actions: global entropy and the local top-2 margin. Online bootstrapped self-improvement then lets the actor absorb critic-assisted behaviour over time.

Evidence. Across three long-horizon interactive benchmarks and several backbones, SAG beats both no-critique and always-on-critique agents on the performance–cost trade-off. On ALFWorld, task success rises from 24.6% to 78.4% at a token budget comparable to ReAct (3.1x normalized token efficiency), and a 7B actor with a 3B critic matches a 14B actor without critique.

Why it matters for CR. It gives CR a concrete escalation signal: compute entropy and top-2 margin over the legal_actions distribution, and call the stronger model or reviewer only when both indicate ambiguity. It has the same shape as the jev-gameplay-decision-router / reflex-jev-selective-control confidence floor, and adds published cost/benefit numbers.

Suggested next step

  • Log entropy and top-2 margin over legal actions at every decision_point, then plot escalation rate against win rate on replayed matches before choosing a threshold.

2. SharedKV-BT: stage-gated typed decisions watch

What it is. Wake, Wagle et al., arXiv:2610.07327 (5 Oct 2026). Each active behaviour-tree node exposes stage-local fields and candidates. Shared-KV scores those candidates in parallel and passes the chosen one to a separate execution system.

Evidence. On robot manipulation, mobile navigation and computer-use tasks, typed decisions are 2.36–4.15x faster than prompt-matched autoregressive decoding. On manipulation, joint decision accuracy goes from 75% to 94% and closed-loop success from 0% to 60%. Replaying fixed scores showed that stage gating prevented out-of-order actions and external postconditions prevented premature completion.

Why it matters for CR. Stage-local candidates amount to legal_actions masking scoped by phase (pick card → pick tile → confirm), and execution and postcondition checks stay outside the model. That is a testable alternative to one flat list of card × tile actions.

Suggested next step

  • Prototype a two-stage CR decision (card first, then a tile from that card’s legal tiles) and compare its accuracy and latency with the flat list on logged states.

3. Judged Useless, Queried Anyway watch

What it is. Zhang, Wan, Yu et al., arXiv:2610.06191 (5 Oct 2026; noted in the 10-06 brief and verified again today). The authors build a retrieval environment with controlled source failures, which lets them separate how agents judge results from what they then do.

Evidence.

  • Judgment without action: where judgments are recorded, the seven agents tested call a failing source’s results useless 97–100% of the time, yet most rarely stop on that judgment.
  • Prompts don’t fix it: prompt cues change when agents stop but not what they stop on, and a stopping rule or call cost written into the prompt is followed only partly.
  • Harness rules do: stopping follows the evidence only when the harness forces an answer after five consecutive results the agent judged useless. That raises failing-source success for every model and keeps the stopping point fixed when the budget doubles.
  • Replication: a preregistered replication on 300 fresh questions confirms both findings.

Why it matters for CR. It’s strong evidence for putting control rules in the harness, not the prompt. A CR agent that keeps picking actions it has just seen rejected, or has flagged itself, should be stopped by a harness rule.

Suggested next step

  • Add a harness rule: after N consecutive rejected or self-flagged actions, force a wait or escalate. Measure repeated-rejection streaks before and after.

4. Learn2Play Bench: games whose rules aren’t in pretraining watch · thin

What it is. Li, Qiu, Zheng et al., arXiv:2610.08215 (6 Oct 2026; project site). Newly designed text games with novel or counterintuitive rules, reproducible feedback and automatic scoring. Game instances vary so transfer can be tested, and the paper compares backbones, self-evolving methods and harnesses.

Evidence. Abstract-level only, with no headline numbers, so this card is marked thin. The three findings:

  • Keeping complete records of actions and feedback supports learning better than summarizing them into rules or strategies.
  • Top human players reach higher peak scores than the agents; humans explore more varied strategies and repeat actions less.
  • With the backbone fixed, changing the harness can improve performance while lowering estimated inference cost.

Why it matters for CR. Mini Royale is a learn-from-play setting too. The paper argues for keeping raw (action → result) history rather than distilled “lessons” (cf. the 10-05 action-outcome card), and the action-repetition rate is a cheap per-match diagnostic.
