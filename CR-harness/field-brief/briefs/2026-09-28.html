1. Game Arena: retry and forfeit rules are part of the benchmark candidate_intent

What it is. Doerschuk-Tiberi et al. (Kaggle + Google DeepMind), arXiv:2609.31473 (25 Sep 2026), with the open harness at google-deepmind/game_arena and the Kaggle launch post. It covers three pilot environments: Chess (perfect information), Poker (imperfect information) and Werewolf (8-player social deduction).

Evidence (from the report).

  • Chess: the environment enforces legality. On an invalid move the harness only says it is invalid; the reason and the alternatives are deliberately hidden. The model gets 3 retries, and a 4th failure is a loss. Retries (“rethinks”) are tracked as a reliability metric, and the report finds weaker models make more illegal moves as games progress. 40 games per pair, colour-balanced; Bradley–Terry Elo with bootstrap CIs.
  • Poker: one retry, then a conservative default (check if no pending bet, otherwise fold). 20,000 hands per pair, 900,000 hands in the ten-model round-robin, BB/100 with block-bootstrap CIs.
  • Werewolf: after the retry cap for parse failures, the harness forces a turn forfeit, which is always legal in that ruleset.

Why it matters for CR. These are exactly the knobs a Mini Royale harness has at each decision_point: whether to show legal_actions (the chess-text leaderboard does not), how much feedback an illegal action gets, the retry budget, and the fallback (loss, conservative noop, or forfeit). The three games pick three different answers, and each changes what the leaderboard measures.

Suggested next step

  • Write CR’s harness policy as a versioned config (list shown? feedback level, retries, fallback = noop/wait) and stamp it on every ETL row.
  • Log rethink/retry counts by match phase (early, mid, overtime) to check whether CR shows the same late-game degradation.
  • Treat “fallback to noop” as its own outcome in reports so it isn’t hidden inside win rate.

2. ContrAgent: one contract for live gating and offline grading watch

What it is. Xiao, Nuzzo et al., arXiv:2609.18128 (16 Sep 2026). An agent’s tool calls become a trace over checkable predicates; required behavior is written as assume-guarantee contracts in LTL over finite traces and compiled to a DFA.

Evidence. The same DFA gates actions online and evaluates recorded traces offline. On four benchmarks the authors report parity with LLM-judge and rule-based guardrail baselines, deterministic verdicts, and orders-of-magnitude lower per-call latency online.

Why it matters for CR. legal_actions answers “is this move legal now?”. Temporal rules (“don’t retry the same rejected action more than N times”, “don’t leave elixir capped for more than T ticks”) need sequence checks. A single artifact that drives both the cockpit gate and the ETL scorer keeps live and offline verdicts from drifting apart.

Suggested next step

  • Draft 2–3 CR temporal rules as tiny automata, run them offline over existing ETL logs first, and only then consider live gating.

3. TC-ECE: calibrate by trajectory checkpoint, not pooled watch

What it is. Lin et al., arXiv:2609.07395 (7 Sep 2026). A three-axis taxonomy of agent uncertainty plus Trajectory-Checkpoint Expected Calibration Error and a reporting protocol.

Evidence. A counterexample shows step-level calibration does not imply trajectory-level calibration. On real traces (four models, up to a 50-step budget), confidence from the agent’s own responses did not consistently beat a simple baseline, and pooling all trajectories hid overconfidence at later stages.

Why it matters for CR. This is a direct caveat for the REFLEX/Jev gate idea (2026-09-25 brief). A confidence floor tuned on pooled decisions can look calibrated while being overconfident in late-match decision_points.

Suggested next step

  • When gate confidence is logged, report ECE and risk–coverage separately for early, mid and late match checkpoints.

4. GameLogicBench: rules can break mid-run watch

What it is. Che et al., arXiv:2609.21562 (18 Sep 2026). 72 Godot gameplay-logic tasks for coding agents, with rules asserted at every simulation tick.

Evidence. 403 hand-designed scenarios, expanded to 1,451 seeded test cases. The evaluator must accept varied correct implementations and reject mutants that each lack one capability. The best of 20 model–scaffold combinations solves 52.78%. Without mutant validation, incorrect submissions passed. With network access open, agents copied code from public repos.

Why it matters for CR. “A game can end in a valid state even after violating its rules during the run” also applies to the Mini Royale sim. If agents or bots edit sim code, end-state checks are not enough.

Suggested next step

  • Add a handful of per-tick invariants (elixir bounds, placement zones, tower HP monotonicity) and prove they bite by running them against deliberately broken sim mutants.
