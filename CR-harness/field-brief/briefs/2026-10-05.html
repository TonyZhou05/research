1. XiangqiBench: finding the move is not winning the game candidate_intent

What it is. Chai, Peng, Xiong et al., arXiv:2610.02425 (submitted 1 Oct 2026). An executable Chinese-chess benchmark. Starting from 119 tactical endgames with forced mates, an LLM agent must actually deliver checkmate against an engine defender. A REPL separates real moves, state queries and forward simulation.

Evidence. 8,568 multi-turn trajectories from 12 frontier LLMs under two observation protocols. Three signals that look like competence each overstate closed-loop success:

  • Conversion gap: models play the stored reference first move in 26.1% of Sighted trials, but only 13.9% of those trials end in a win.
  • Consistency gap: the leading model reaches 38.7% pass@3 but only 5.9% pass^3 (winning all three trials on 7 of the 46 positions it ever wins).
  • Simulation gap: 32.3% of accepted simulation calls stop on an illegal move, and in 49.3% of comparable cases the real defender replies differently from the line the agent simulated.

Why it matters for CR. This is the game-domain version of the 10-01 scoping-review point. Per-decision_point “good move” scores can look healthy while matches are still lost. The REPL split (act / query / simulate) is also a clean interface pattern for Mini Royale.

Suggested next step (intent candidate)

  • Report pass^k (win all k seeds) next to pass@k for every CR model–harness config.
  • If agents get a lookahead/simulate tool, log illegal actions inside the lookahead separately from real-action legality.
  • Keep decision-level metrics, but make closed-loop match outcome the headline.

2. Fast Models, Slow Evidence: auditing System-1 decision models watch

What it is. Li et al., arXiv:2610.02267 (1 Oct 2026; cases, outputs and code). A paired evaluation of an open-weight (Laya) and a hosted (Jev) System-1 typed-decision model on 11 agent decision points built from 18 public sources: 7,283 base cases plus 6,640 robustness variants, byte-identical inputs, and cross-hardware and cross-day reproducibility checks.

Evidence. Jev is significantly more accurate on 9 of 11 decision points (+10.8 to +46.0 pp). Neither beats chance on zero-shot model routing, and they tie on RAG relevance gating. Laya changes 30% of its answers when option order is reversed and falls to 31% with 50 nearest-neighbour tools, versus 98% for Jev on items with a unique correct tool. The authors also audit themselves and report four errors that distorted deployment claims: an omitted pre-screen cost (23.9% saving reported, 4.3% actual), gate accuracy reported as end-to-end quality (58% vs 98%), in-sample thresholds (5% target, up to 17% held-out misses), and a “channel effect” that vanished with channel-native content.

Why it matters for CR. It is the best pre-flight checklist yet for the jev-gameplay-decision-router intent. Mini Royale legal_actions lists are long and near-duplicate (same card, neighbouring tiles), which is exactly where order bias and many-similar-candidate failures show up.

Suggested next step

  • Before trusting a confidence floor: shuffle legal-action order and check answer stability, test with 50+ near-identical placements, set the floor on held-out matches, and count every pre-screen call in the cost.

3. Label observations as outcomes of the preceding action watch · thin

What it is. Liu, Wang, Jing et al., arXiv:2610.02769 (2 Oct 2026). A study of how language agents use interaction history within a task.

Evidence. History helps overall, but much of the benefit persists when past actions are shuffled, and breaking the action–observation correspondence costs only a modest drop. That suggests agents don’t reliably link actions to outcomes. Explicitly labeling each observation as the outcome of the preceding action raises task success and reduces next-action repetition without new information, and a learned calibrator that reassesses past actions adds more. The abstract gives no benchmark names or numbers and I didn’t read the full paper, so this card is marked thin.

Why it matters for CR. It’s a near-free prompt change for the decision_point history block, and it targets a known failure: repeating an action that was just rejected.

Suggested next step

  • Render recent history as action → result pairs (e.g. “deploy X at tile T → rejected: insufficient elixir”) and compare the repeated-rejected-action rate against the current format.

4. OpenGameEval: make exploration measurable watch

What it is. Turkel, Sun, Ayyar et al. (Roblox), arXiv:2610.02563 (1 Oct 2026; MIT release). Agents do game development in reproducible, stateful Roblox Studio sessions, scored by executable checks on the edited scene and in a simulated play session. The eight-tool action space separates observation tools from editing tools.

Evidence. 13 frontier models, 84 human-curated tasks, 16 attempts each. The best model solves 51.7% on a single attempt and 39.4% five times out of five, and no model solves six tasks. Holding task and model fixed, runs that inspect every object the reference solution touches before acting pass 13.4 pp more often on scene-only tasks and 9.8 pp on script-only tasks than runs that inspect none.

Why it matters for CR. The agents build games rather than play them, but two design points transfer: separate observe/query actions from acting actions so “looked before acting” is loggable, and report k-of-k reliability.

Suggested next step

  • If CR adds query tools (e.g. inspect a lane, check an opponent card cycle), keep them out of legal_actions and log them as a separate channel.
