1 · SO-Arena: strong System One accuracy does not mean reliable decisions

watch arXiv:2611.15777 (submitted 11 Nov 2026; Li, Dong, Feng, Li, Wang). The authors introduce SO-Arena, a System One benchmark of 2,919 tasks across 11 domains and five capability families, mixing 2,300 static decisions with 282 tool-use episodes in native simulators and 337 text-based web tasks. Besides accuracy, they score decision reliability in two ways: whether the decision stays stable when the option set is changed in a controlled way (including reordering), and whether the model recognizes that no valid option is present when the correct option is removed. Across 21 models, they report that the gains from scaling and extra reasoning vary a lot by domain, and — more to the point for a typed menu — that the best-performing System One model ranks only seventh on reliability. A public site and dataset accompany the paper.

For CR: legal_actions lists have an arbitrary order, and sometimes the right move is to abstain or escalate rather than pick from a bad menu. SO-Arena turns those two properties into a scored reliability axis, separate from task success. That is a template for logging permutation flip rate and missing-option recognition at decision_point, next to the IJev card already on the board.

Suggested next step: On a fixed set of saved CR decision points, permute the legal-action list and also drop the chosen action from the menu; report flip rate and whether the model abstains or invents when no good option remains.

2 · Jev-style confidence is a dispersion formula, not accuracy

watch arXiv:2611.12521 (submitted 9 Nov 2026; Delaunay). An open audit of five open-weight System 1 models (0.8B to 27B) plus generative System 2 baselines. The authors report that the provider confidence field is an exact closed-form dispersion transform — one minus normalized Shannon entropy on the sub-10B models, and a normalized margin on Clef-27B — rather than empirical accuracy. Bundling several questions in one request caused directional cross-question interference (11.7%–22.4% binary flips on Tev1-0.8B, versus 0.1%–0.2% on Tev1-4B). In the paper body, permuting the option list caused 11.1% flips on Tev1-0.8B and 2.9% on Tev1-4B. Models with at least 4B parameters were faster and better calibrated under adversarial syntactic shifts (ECEw 0.030–0.087) than single-token log-probability (0.144) and structured JSON (0.378) System 2 baselines. Code is at j2launay/jev-decision-models-xai.

For CR: The fleet already gates on a Jev confidence_floor. If that field is normalized entropy, not P(correct), the floor should be set and reported against max probability or a calibrated score, not the raw provider number. Isolated (not bundled) questions at decision_point, and a permutation flip-rate check, belong in the eval next to IJev.

Suggested next step: Log both provider confidence and max probability on CR decision points, and compare which one better ranks correct versus incorrect choices before touching the floor.

3 · S1-Evo: untrusted context can flip typed System-1 choices, including through the confidence gate

watch arXiv:2611.16937 (submitted 12 Nov 2026; Wang, Li, Zhou, Lou, Li). The authors argue that attacks written for token-by-token System-2 models do not match typed System-1 interfaces, which score rules and options in one pass and often sit behind a confidence gate. S1-Evo perturbs untrusted fields so that injected evidence competes with the original task evidence, distills verified flips from seed tasks into experience cards, and retrieves them at test time. Across four benchmarks and three S1 models, they report 71.99% mean attack success and 57.47% high-confidence attack success on jev-1.14, and up to 24.00% end-to-end agent violations in two dual-system workflows.

For CR: If Jev or another typed gate sits on decision_point, the legal menu and rules are not the only inputs that matter. An untrusted observation, tool return, or note can move both the choice and the confidence over the floor. Treat those fields as an attack surface in ETL, and score high-confidence flips, not only raw accuracy.

Suggested next step: In replay, append a short untrusted note to otherwise clean observations and count how often the gated choice changes while reported confidence stays above the floor. No need to reproduce the authors’ search procedure.

4 · MMDM: knowledge is easier than option logic, constraints, and complete multi-select

watch arXiv:2611.14309 (submitted 10 Nov 2026; Shen, Zong, Guo, Zhang, Guo, Cheng). MMDM is a 5,448-question System-1 benchmark across 16 task categories, with single-select, multi-select, and soft-label items, option spaces from 2 to 1,024, and inputs up to 64,817 tokens, including agent state tracking and tool/skill selection. Evaluating 28 decision models, the authors report that models can do well on common knowledge yet struggle with interdependent options, constrained decisions, and recovering a complete valid set: Perplexity Decider-V1.2 reaches 78.75% Hard Single-select accuracy on world knowledge but only 53.95% on option logic and 47.95% on constraint planning, and the best Hard Multi-select accuracy across all models is 36.81%. The dataset and toolkit are public at mmdm-benchmark.github.io.

For CR: A legal_actions menu can be large and constrained (elixir, cycle, already-played). Picking the single top action is not the same as recovering every legal or every useful one, or checking that a plan still satisfies the constraints. MMDM is a public yardstick for those failure modes, next to SO-Arena’s reliability axis.

Suggested next step: When a CR state has several jointly legal useful actions, score whether a typed selector ranks all of them above the illegal or dominated ones, not only whether argmax is a legal move.
